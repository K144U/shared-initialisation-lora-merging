\paragraph{LoRA and its initialisation.} A LoRA adapter replaces a weight
update by $\Delta W = BA$ with $B \in \R^{m\times r}$, $A \in \R^{r\times d}$
and $r \ll \min(m, d)$~\citep{hu2022lora}. In the reference implementation and
in PEFT, $B$ starts at zero and $A$ is drawn from a Kaiming-uniform
distribution using the global random generator. The two factors play
different roles: $A$ mostly selects input features and $B$ produces the
output. A random untrained $A$ performs almost as well as a trained
one~\citep{zhu2024asymmetry}. Several designs share $A$ on purpose.
HydraLoRA~\citep{tian2024hydralora} uses one $A$ for several $B$ heads.
Federated variants either freeze $A$, which makes aggregation exact
(FFA-LoRA~\citep{sun2024ffalora}), or share only $A$ across clients, on the
grounds that it carries the general part of what is learned
(FedSA-LoRA~\citep{guo2025fedsalora}). Other initialisations reach a shared
$A$ without a shared seed. PiSSA~\citep{meng2024pissa},
OLoRA~\citep{buyukakyuz2024olora} and MiLoRA~\citep{wang2025milora} compute
$A$ from the base weight, so every task fine-tuned from one base with the same
modules and rank starts from the same $A$. CorDA~\citep{yang2024corda} orients
its decomposition by sampled data, which gives every task the same start when
the samples are the same. LoRA-FA~\citep{zhang2023lorafa} freezes a random
$A$, which is shared only when the seed is. We found no study of what such
cohorts do to a merge.

\paragraph{Merging methods, by what they compute.} We sort merging methods by
the operation they apply to the adapters. \emph{Averaging and
sparsifying} methods form a weighted combination of the task updates. They
include task arithmetic~\citep{ilharco2023task}, TIES~\citep{yadav2023ties},
DARE~\citep{yu2024dare}, DELLA~\citep{della2024} and the quantised
TVQ~\citep{kim2025tvq}. \emph{Subspace} methods act on the singular structure
of the updates. KnOTS~\citep{stoica2025knots} aligns the updates in a shared
basis and then combines them with an averaging or sparsifying rule (we use its
TIES variant). It inverts nothing, so we group it with those methods.
TSV-M~\citep{gargiulo2025tsv} whitens the stacked singular vectors of the
updates. \emph{Solvers} find the merged weight as the solution of a linear
system. RegMean~\citep{jin2023regmean} solves a least-squares problem in
per-task Gram matrices of the input activations. MaTS~\citep{tam2024mats}
solves a system in a Fisher surrogate by conjugate gradient.
RegMean++~\citep{regmeanpp2025} and LoRM~\citep{lorm2024} extend the closed
form. WUDI-Merging~\citep{cheng2025wudi} needs no data. It uses the rows of
each task update as stand-in inputs for that task, so its least-squares
matrix is $\sum_t \Delta W_t^\top \Delta W_t / \|\Delta W_t\|_F^2$, built from
the updates themselves. Its authors minimise the objective with a few hundred
gradient steps instead of solving the system. Fisher
merging~\citep{matena2022fisher} weights each parameter by a diagonal Fisher
estimate. For this paper, what matters about a solver is where its matrix
comes from. It can come from data, as in RegMean and MaTS, or from the
adapters themselves, as in WUDI and in a data-free form of RegMean. We
construct that data-free form by replacing each activation Gram with
$A_t^\top A_t$.

\paragraph{Shared initialisation in weight-space merging.} For full models, a
shared starting point is what makes weight averaging work. Two copies of a
network trained from the same weights with different SGD noise end, once past
an early point of training, in minima joined by a segment of low
loss~\citep{frankle2020lmc}. Models fine-tuned from one pre-trained checkpoint
stay in one basin~\citep{neyshabur2020transfer}, which is why averaging them,
as model soups do~\citep{wortsman2022soups}, improves accuracy instead of
destroying it. Models trained from different initialisations must first be
aligned, for example by permuting hidden units as Git
Re-Basin~\citep{ainsworth2023rebasin} does. The adapters of a LoRA cohort
share their base model by construction. The shared starting point we study is
a second one, the random draw of $A$. It is harmless to averaging, consistent
with this literature, but it collapses the subspaces that a solver has to tell
apart, and no alignment undoes it (\S\ref{subsec:audit}).
LoRI~\citep{zhang2025lori} freezes each task's $A$ as an independent random
matrix, so that the adapters are approximately orthogonal, and argues that
this limits interference when they are merged. The argument assumes that the
draws are independent, which a shared seed violates without any visible sign.
ZipLoRA~\citep{shah2024ziplora} observes that highly aligned columns of two
LoRA updates merge poorly and learns per-column coefficients that reduce the
alignment. The overlap that a shared draw creates lies in the input subspaces
and is close to total, and in our experiments it breaks only the merges that
solve against it.

\paragraph{Analyses of merging.} Weight
disentanglement~\citep{ortizjimenez2023disentanglement} explains when task
arithmetic works: distinct directions in weight space should control separate,
local regions of function space. The operator we define below is a simple
model in that spirit for LoRA, in which each adapter acts only through its own
input subspace. Cao et al.~\citep{cao2026collapse} report that conflict
metrics in parameter space predict little about when merging fails, while
representational incompatibility between tasks predicts a great deal, and
prove a limit for convex merges. We agree that geometry in parameter space
does not affect averaging-type merges (\S\ref{sec:merging}). We add that one
such quantity does predict how solvers built from the adapters behave; these
solvers are not convex combinations of the fine-tuned models. A systematic
study of merging heterogeneous fine-tuned checkpoints on four open-weight
language models~\citep{systematic2025merging} finds that only task arithmetic
reliably improves on the base model. That result concerns which methods work
on average; ours concerns a property of the cohort that decides whether one
class of them works.

\paragraph{Notation.} A cohort has $T$ adapters $\{(A_t, B_t)\}$ of rank $r$
on a common base. The input subspace of adapter $t$ in a layer is the row
space $V_t = \range(A_t^\top) \subset \R^d$, and $P_t$ is the orthogonal
projector onto it. We measure the overlap between two task subspaces
$V_i, V_j$ by the cosines of their principal angles. We report the median of
these cosines over pairs and over a sample of layers. We model the curvature
a solver sees in a layer by the projectors onto the adapters' input
subspaces, so the operator it must invert is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hbar}
  \bar H = \frac1T \sum_{t=1}^T P_t.
\end{equation}
The exact interpolator is the minimum-norm update that reproduces every task
update on its own subspace. It is $\tauH = \bar D\,\bar H^{+}$, with
$\bar D = \tfrac1T\sum_t \Delta W_t$ and $(\cdot)^+$ the pseudoinverse. We
report two quantities, both computed from the adapter weights alone. The
condition number $\kappa(\bar H)$ is the ratio of the largest to the smallest
nonzero eigenvalue of $\bar H$. The \emph{amplification}
$\|\tauH\| / \|\bar D\|$ says how much larger the exact interpolator is than
the mean update.

\paragraph{Ill-conditioned and ill-posed.} The exact interpolator asks for one
update that reproduces each task's update on that task's subspace. If the
subspaces coincide, tasks that ask for different outputs on the same inputs
cannot all be satisfied, and the pseudoinverse returns their least-squares
compromise: with every $P_t$ equal to one projector $P$, $\bar H = P$ and
$\tauH = \bar D P = \bar D$, the mean update. A collapsed cohort lies close to
this case, because its subspaces differ only by small tilts that training
added to a common start. An interpolator still exists, but it reproduces the
tasks' differences through the differences between the tilts, so it grows
without bound as the tilts shrink (\S\ref{sec:theory}), while at coincidence
it is $\bar D$. The solution therefore does not depend continuously on the
adapters at that point, so the problem is ill-posed there in the sense of
Hadamard~\citep{engl1996regularization}. A collapsed cohort is a small
perturbation of this case, which is why $\kappa(\bar H)$ is large. Solving more
accurately does not help, because the large solution is what the problem asks
for, not an error in computing it. In float64 our encoder's merge fails as it
does in float32 (\S\ref{sec:merging}). What helps is asking a different
question. The mean update, which averaging methods form without inverting
anything, is the least-squares answer at the limit that a collapsed cohort
approaches. A ridge, the standard treatment of ill-posed
problems~\citep{engl1996regularization}, damps the directions in which the
tilts differ, where the solution is unstable.
