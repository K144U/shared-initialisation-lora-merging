We evaluate the loss from the
factors rather than from dense matrices. It is the same function, and we
checked its value, its gradient and a $50$-step Adam trajectory against the
dense form.

The account we registered of how this happens did not hold. We predicted that
the closed form would put at least half of its energy in the bottom quarter of
$\bar H$'s spectrum, as the two solvers of \S\ref{subsec:results} do. It puts
$0.03$ to $0.17$ there on the shared arm, less than on the independent arm. We
also predicted that the 3000-step arm would fall between the other two. It does
not on two of the four bases: it lies above the closed form on Qwen and below
the published version on Yi. Two diagnostics separate the arms for the
published procedure. We recorded them but did not register them as mediators.
The first is how far Adam moves. The starting sum has exactly $T = 4$ times
the norm of the mean update. On independent cohorts Adam leaves it there
(amplification $4.0$), and on shared cohorts it drives it to $8.6$ to $12.5$.
The second is where the movement goes. On the shared arm, $24$ to $27\%$ of
the merged update's squared norm lies outside the row space of the $A_t$,
against $0.8$ to $1.6\%$ on the independent arm. The loss in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:wudi} cannot see that component, because it depends on $\tau$
only through $\tau D_t^\top$. One reading fits both diagnostics. On
near-orthogonal subspaces the sum already minimises the loss, since
$D_sD_t^\top \approx 0$ for $s \neq t$, so there is little gradient. On a
collapsed cohort the sum counts the shared directions about four times, the
loss is far from its minimum, and Adam moves far. Adam rescales each
coordinate separately, so its steps leave the row space, and the loss never
corrects what lands outside it. We offer this as a description, not as a
tested mechanism.

\subsection{The heuristics: equivalence margins and the lean}\label{app:lean}

The registered equivalence margins were $3$ points on GSM8K and $5$ on
HumanEval. Each of the five heuristics would pass at $0.9$ to $2.0$ points
and at $2.1$ to $3.0$ points respectively.

We also registered that a slight lean towards the shared arm, seen at one
cohort per arm, would vanish at three. It persisted: $29$ of the $38$ non-zero
differences favour the shared arm (sign test, $p = 0.0017$). That test treats
the $40$ cells as independent, although the ten cells of a base share its six
cohorts and its evaluation sets. We ran a permutation test afterwards that
relabels whole cohorts within each base. It gives $p = 0.007$ for a mean lean
of $0.7$ points, with all four bases leaning the same way. We tested one
explanation in advance. Truncation to rank $r$ discards more of an independent
cohort's merged update: $12$ to $25\%$ of task arithmetic's squared norm,
against $1.3$ to $1.6\%$ on the shared arm, and more on the independent arm for
every heuristic on every base. Merging without truncation should then have
halved the lean. It did not halve on any of the three method-benchmark pairs
where the lean was largest (task arithmetic and DARE at HumanEval, TIES at
GSM8K), so truncation is excluded as its cause. No heuristic enters the
ill-conditioned directions, so the lean is not the solver mechanism either,
and we have no tested explanation for it.

\subsection{Numerical precision and truncation}\label{app:precision}

Both solvers run in float32,
and at $\lambda = 10^{-6}$ and $\lambda = 0$ their systems are badly
conditioned. The failure could therefore in principle be rounding error. We
registered a check before running it. For each base, arm and solver, we
recomputed the merge in float64 from the same adapter factors and compared the
two after exact truncation to rank $r$. Precision would be excluded if the
median difference over modules stayed below $1\%$ and the ratio of shared to
independent amplification changed by less than a factor of $2$. The ratios
agree within $0.1\%$ in every case, and $15$ of the $16$ medians are below
$1\%$, at most $0.9\%$. The encoder on the shared Yi cohort is at $1.09\%$
and fails. As registered, we rebuilt that merge in float64 and evaluated it
again. Its worst-task excess is $0.245$ nats, against $0.249$ in float32 and
$0.121$ on the independent cohort, so rounding does not cause the failure.
Runs we added afterwards show that the float32 merge is unstable instead. The
encoder's per-task bases come from a randomised SVD, and the unregularised
solve amplifies its rounding. Float32 merges that differ only in the random
seed reach worst-task excesses of $0.22$ to $0.28$ nats on this cohort, and
$0.26$ to $0.84$ when the truncation to rank $r$ is exact. All of them lie
above the independent cohort, whose float32 merge agrees with float64 to
$2\times10^{-6}$ in the median over modules. On a shared cohort of each other
base, three draws under the same seeds span $0.31$ to $0.33$ nats on Llama,
$9.78$ to $9.86$ on Mistral and $0.19$ to $0.22$ on Qwen. We then drew the
two other shared cohorts of every base three times each. The draws of one
cohort span up to $0.08$ nats on Llama, $0.15$ on Qwen and $0.23$ on Yi, and
up to $1.1$ on Mistral, whose excess is about $10$ nats
(Table~\ref{tab:appC-sweep}). All $36$ draws lie above the independent arm.
On one cohort, Yi's third, the spread is larger than the distance from its
lowest draw to the independent mean ($0.23$ against $0.10$); on the others it
is smaller. The float32 values we report for the unregularised encoder on
shared cohorts are single draws of this kind. Their direction has not
depended on the draw, but their size does. On Yi the ratio of the shared to
the independent arm at $\lambda = 0$ ranges from $1.8$ to $2.6$ over the
draws, so the registered factor of two holds on the registered cells but not
on every draw.
Adapter-only RegMean agrees with float64 to within $1\%$ on every module.

Every merge in this paper is cut to rank $r$ by a randomised SVD that computes
$r$ vectors with four power iterations and no oversampling. On the merges of
the two solvers it keeps $98.8$ to $99.9\%$ of the norm of the exact
truncation, but not its directions. In relative Frobenius norm it is a median
$30$ to $38\%$ from the exact rank-$r$ truncation on the shared arm, and $12$
to $20\%$ on the independent arm. Both arms are cut the same way, and the
encoder's failure survives with this step removed (\S\ref{subsec:results}).

\subsection{All four tasks}\label{app:alltasks}

The accuracy results so far cover mathematics and
code. We registered tests on the other two tasks: WMT19 English-to-German
(chrF on $500$ pairs) and IFEval (strict prompt-level accuracy on $300$
prompts). They use the seed-1 and independent-1 cohorts and our encoder, whose
merges at $\lambda = 0$ and at the selected $\lambda^\star$ were already
evaluated on the other tasks. On translation the collapse is visible. On the
shared cohort the selected ridge beats $\lambda = 0$ by $13.9$, $55.1$, $2.9$
and $6.5$ chrF points on the four bases, past the gate of $2$ on all four.
Task arithmetic and TIES differ across arms by at most $1.1$ points. On IFEval
the collapse is not visible. The ridge gains $0.080$, $0.333$, $0.043$ and
$0.040$, past the gate on Mistral only. Llama misses by $0.001$ and is counted
as a miss, as registered. The heuristics again differ by at most $0.057$,
inside every gate. The reference rows explain part of this. The instruction
adapter alone does not beat the base model on IFEval on three of four bases
(Llama scores $0.627$ against the base model's $0.703$), so there is little
task skill for a broken merge to lose. The translation adapter is also below
the base model on chrF on three bases, because of the data-loading defect of
\S\ref{subsec:setup}. What the unregularised merge destroys on translation is
therefore mostly the base model's own ability to translate.

\subsection{Setting the ridge without data}\label{subsec:lambda}

A ridge repairs both solvers built from the adapter factors on most bases, but
the amount needed does not track the conditioning. We tested three data-free
rules for choosing it, and all three failed. The first sets $\lambda$ to bring
the adapter Gram to a target condition number, chosen in advance and evaluated
only on unseen cohorts. It was worse than a fixed $\lambda = 0.01$ on every
collapsed cohort tested. On separated cohorts it met its registered criterion
of doing no harm on $4$ of $6$; the two misses were cohorts where it did
better than the minimal ridge. On collapsed public cohorts it brought the
solver within $1.5$ times task arithmetic's excess on $2$ of $6$, against a
registered $80\%$. The fixed $\lambda = 0.01$, registered to do the same on at
least $80\%$ of collapsed public cohorts, did so on $1$ of $6$.
Adapter-only RegMean is a ridge regression whose samples and targets can both
be read off the adapter files. The classical rules for choosing the parameter
therefore apply as they stand, and we registered two of them: generalised
cross-validation (GCV) and the L-curve. Neither repaired the collapse on more
than one base of four. Neither passed any of its three other registered
criteria (no worse than $\lambda = 0.01$, near the best of the sweep, no harm
on separated cohorts). GCV raised the independent cohort's excess on Llama
from $0.07$ to $0.19$. On the first shared cohort of each base, GCV chose
$\lambda$ near $10^{-4}$ and left the worst-task excess at $1.13$, $8.66$,
$0.22$ and $0.45$, against $0.11$, $0.05$, $0.01$ and $0.02$ at the best
$\lambda$ of the sweep. On Llama, Qwen and Yi that is worse than the minimal
ridge. The L-curve found no interior corner. On every base and arm its median
choice was $1.3\times10^{-8}$, at the lower edge of a grid that starts at
$10^{-8}$. A data-free way to set the ridge remains open. We recommend
starting at $0.01$ and sweeping on held-out data.

\subsection{Merges of public cohorts}\label{subsec:against}

We merged the
public cohorts that a fixed rule selected: causal language models of at most
$15$B parameters that load without custom code, with members sharing rank and
target modules. The rule selected seven collapsed and thirteen separated
cohorts, and one of the collapsed cohorts could not be loaded. Public adapters
rarely ship their data, so the consequence measure scored each adapter's own
greedy completions on a fixed prompt set under the merged model.
Unregularised adapter-only RegMean at least doubles task arithmetic's excess
on $5$ of $6$ collapsed cohorts, and also on $11$ of $13$ separated ones
(Fisher's exact test, $p = 0.78$). The solver fails often on public cohorts,
but not more often when they are collapsed. These merges therefore show how
common the collapse is (\S\ref{sec:prevalence}), not what it costs on public
cohorts.

\paragraph{Two candidate causes, tested on our own cohorts.} Public cohorts
differ from ours in ways a fixed rule cannot control, so we injected each
difference into our separated cohorts on Llama and Qwen. A LoRA update is
unchanged when $A$ is multiplied by $4$ and $B$ divided by $4$, but $A^\top A$
is not. That rescaling changes adapter-only RegMean's excess by at most $4\%$,
below the gate, so this freedom in the factorisation is not the cause. Putting
every adapter in a balanced form changes nothing either. Scale mismatch does
matter, which we did not predict. Making one adapter's update $4$ times larger
multiplies RegMean's excess by $3.9$ and $5.3$ on the two bases, while task
arithmetic's excess falls to $0.26$ and $0.29$ of its unperturbed value. Scale
mismatch between adapters is therefore a hazard specific to solvers, which we
observed on separated cohorts. It does not explain the public data, however:
the spread of $A$ norms within a cohort is no larger where the solver failed
than where it did not. The public failures remain unexplained.
